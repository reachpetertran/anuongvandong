## Key points

- Slow breathing means deliberately slowing your breath, usually to about **6 breaths a minute** (10 seconds per breath). The NHS (UK) suggests breathing in while counting 1–5, out while counting 1–5, for **at least 5 minutes**. [Verified]
- Reviews of RCTs link breathing exercises with a **small-to-moderate reduction in stress**. Slow breathing **lowers systolic blood pressure right after practice**; long-term effects are unclear. [Verified]
- Slow breathing **does not replace medicines** for high blood pressure, anxiety or any other condition. [Verified]

## How to do it – the minimal version

**Step 1. Get comfortable.** Sit in a chair with back support, feet flat on the floor. Or lie down, or stand. Loosen tight clothing. [Verified]

**Step 2. Set a 5-minute timer.** [Verified]

**Step 3. Breathe in gently through your nose, counting 1 to 5.** Let the breath reach your belly as far as is comfortable, without forcing it. It is fine if you cannot reach 5 at first. [Verified]

**Step 4. Breathe out gently, counting 1 to 5.** Do not hold your breath in between. [Verified]

**Step 5. Repeat until the timer ends.** In for 5 seconds, out for 5 seconds is about 6 breaths a minute. If that is hard, 4 in and 4 out is fine. [Inference]

**Step 6. Do it at a fixed time.** For example after waking up, or at lunch. [Inference]

> Hypothetical example: at 12:30 every day, sit at your desk, set a 5-minute timer and breathe with a 5–5 count. [Hypothetical example]

## Common misunderstandings

- **"Slow breathing cures high blood pressure, so I don't need medicine."** The AHA lists slow breathing as an add-on, not a replacement for standard treatment. A 2024 review found an immediate fall in blood pressure; the effect over months is unclear. [Verified]
- **"The deeper and harder, the better."** The NHS says not to force your breath. A leaflet from an NHS hospital notes you may feel a little dizzy at first because you are breathing more deeply than usual. [Verified]
- **"Fast breathing and long breath-holds work better."** In the Fincham 2023 review, only 2 studies mainly used fast breathing – too few to compare. The authors say the safety of fast breathwork needs more research. This site does not teach these techniques. [Verified]

## Who should take care

- **People with lung or heart conditions** or another chronic condition: ask a health worker before starting. NHS Scotland gives this advice to people with breathing problems. [Verified]
- **Never over-breathe (hyperventilate) and then hold your breath in or near water.** It can cause blackout underwater from lack of oxygen, and drowning. [Verified]
- **Do not practise while driving or operating machinery** if the exercise makes you drowsy or light-headed. [Expert judgement]
- **People taking blood-pressure medicines or sedatives:** do not reduce or stop medicines because you are doing breathing exercises. [Expert judgement]
- If focusing on your breath makes you **more anxious**, stop and try something else, such as a slow walk. [Expert judgement]

## When to see a health worker

- You are often short of breath, even at rest. [Expert judgement]
- Dizziness, tingling hands or feet, or a racing heart that does not settle once you breathe normally again. [Expert judgement]
- Stress or anxiety that lasts and affects your work, sleep or daily life. [Expert judgement]
- Chest pain, severe breathlessness or fainting: call emergency services (see [Find help](/en/find-help/)).

## Level of evidence

**Low to moderate.** [Expert judgement]

- **Fincham 2023, Scientific Reports** (review of RCTs): 12 RCTs with 785 adults measured stress: a small-to-moderate reduction versus controls (Hedges' g = −0.35). Anxiety (20 RCTs) g = −0.32; depression (18 RCTs) g = −0.40. Most studies had moderate risk of bias; few had long-term follow-up. [Verified]
- **Shao 2024, Mindfulness** (31 studies, 1,133 people without a clinical condition): slow breathing lowered systolic blood pressure right after practice (SMD −0.45) and raised heart-rate variability. The effect on stress was only borderline. Long-term cardiovascular effects are unclear. [Verified]
- These figures are standardised effect sizes, not mmHg or percentages. [Verified]
