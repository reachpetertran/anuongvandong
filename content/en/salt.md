## Key points

- WHO recommends adults eat **less than 2,000 mg of sodium a day, equal to less than 5 g of salt**. [Verified]
- Vietnamese adults aged 18–69 ate on average **8.07 g of salt a day (2021)**, down from 9.42 g (2015) – still above that level. [Verified]
- Sodium is not only in table salt. Fish sauce, soy sauce, seasoning powder and processed foods all contain it. [Verified]

## What the standards say

Standards use different units: some count **sodium (mg)**, others **salt (g)**. Conversion: salt ≈ sodium × 2.5. [Verified]

| Standard | Adults should eat | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| WHO (fact sheet updated 11 May 2026) | Less than 2,000 mg sodium/day (less than 5 g salt) | All salt eaten should be iodized. |
| Vietnam – Ministry of Health (Decision 3594/QĐ-BYT, 29 Nov 2024) | Recommendation 7: limit foods high in salt | The summary of the ten recommendations gives no gram figure. A gram figure for ages 18–64 in the technical guidance: not yet verified. |
| US – DGA 2025–2030 | Less than 2,300 mg sodium/day (age 14 and over) | Highly active people who sweat a lot may need more sodium. |
| Australia – NHMRC (2017) | Target of 2,000 mg sodium/day | A target for the population average. NHMRC consulted on an update (closed 30 Sep 2026). |
| UK – NHS | No more than 6 g salt/day (age 11 and over) | About 1 level teaspoon. |

Why do the standards differ? Partly because they serve different purposes. For example, NHMRC states that Australia's figure is a target for the population average. [Verified] The specific reasons behind each standard: not yet verified.

## How to do it – the minimal version

**Step 1. Read the sodium label on your own condiments.**
Find the "Sodium" line, usually in mg per serving or per 100 ml. Multiply by 2.5 to get grams of salt.

> Hypothetical example (not a real product): the label says 1,000 mg sodium per 15 ml. Then 15 ml holds about 1,000 × 2.5 = 2,500 mg, i.e. 2.5 g of salt. Three such spoons already pass 5 g. [Hypothetical example]

**Step 2. Write down one day of eating.**
List the salty seasonings you use: fish sauce, soy sauce, seasoning powder, stock granules, instant noodles, braised dishes. Use the labels to convert to grams of salt. This is a self-estimate, less accurate than a urine test. [Inference]

**Step 3. Cut at the table first.**
Don't add extra fish sauce. Don't finish the broth of phở, bún or noodles. Keep condiment bottles off the table. [Inference]

**Step 4. Cut gradually when cooking.**
Season a little less each week. Add flavour with lime, garlic, ginger, pepper and herbs. [Inference – the NHS also suggests herbs and spices instead of salt]

If you still use table salt, WHO suggests you may replace it with **lower-sodium salt that contains potassium** (a conditional recommendation). Read "Who should take care" before switching. [Verified]

## Common misunderstandings

- **"I don't sprinkle salt, so I don't eat much."** In Vietnam, fish sauce, soy sauce and other sodium-based condiments are major sources. [Verified]
- **"If it doesn't taste salty, it's low in salt."** WHO notes that bread, processed meat and snack foods can be high in sodium. [Verified]
- **"Cutting salt out completely is best."** The standards above all set an upper limit, not zero. The aim is to get below the limit. [Inference] WHO advises that salt you do eat should be iodized. [Verified]

## Who should take care

- **People with kidney disease, or a condition or medicine that makes it hard to clear potassium:** do not switch to potassium salt on your own. WHO excludes this group from its recommendation. [Verified]
- **Pregnant women and children:** WHO's potassium-salt recommendation does not apply. Children need less salt than adults. [Verified]
- **Heavy manual workers in hot places who sweat a lot:** sodium needs may differ. Ask the health worker at your workplace. [Inference]
- **People on a diet prescribed by a doctor** (high blood pressure, heart failure, kidney disease): follow your doctor, not this article.

## When to see a health worker

- You have never had your blood pressure measured, or not for a long time.
- You have kidney disease, heart disease, high blood pressure or diabetes and want to change how much salt you eat.
- Danger signs such as a drooping face, weakness on one side or slurred speech: call emergency services now (see [Find help](/en/find-help/)).

## Level of evidence

**High** for reducing sodium to below 2 g/day (WHO grades it a strong recommendation). Switching to potassium salt is a **conditional recommendation** from WHO, with a narrower scope. The 8.07 g figure is an estimate from spot urine samples, not 24-hour collection. [Verified]
