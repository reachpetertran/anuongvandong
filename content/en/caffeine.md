## Key points

- Europe (EFSA), the US (FDA) and the UK (FSA) all use a reference of **about 400 mg of caffeine a day** for healthy adults who are not pregnant. [Verified]
- Caffeine is in coffee, tea, milk tea, cola, energy drinks, chocolate and some gym pills and powders. **Add it all up** across the day. [Verified]
- Just **100 mg close to bedtime** may shorten and disturb sleep in some adults (EFSA). [Verified]

## What the standards say

| Standard | Healthy adults | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Vietnam | No verified recommendation on caffeine intake for adults found | – |
| WHO | No recommendation on caffeine intake found | – |
| Europe – EFSA (2015) | Up to **400 mg a day**, spread across the day, raises no safety concern | Single doses up to **200 mg** raise no concern. **100 mg** close to bedtime may affect sleep in some adults. |
| US – FDA (updated 28 Aug 2024) | **400 mg a day** is not generally associated with negative effects | About 2–3 cups of coffee of 355 ml. Tolerance varies by person and by medicines taken. |
| Australia – FSANZ (updated 26 Nov 2025) | **No** official health-based guidance value (such as an ADI) | Gives a reference of about **240 mg a day** for adults, derived from the level linked to anxiety in children (3 mg/kg). Energy drinks: at most **320 mg per litre**. |
| UK – FSA | Up to **400 mg a day** for healthy adults | Pregnancy: at most 200 mg a day. |

The standards do not conflict much. Australia is more cautious because it does not recognise an official "safe" level. [Inference]

## How much caffeine is in a drink

| Drink | Caffeine | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 mug of instant coffee | about 100 mg | NHS |
| 1 mug of tea (black or green) | about 75 mg | NHS |
| 1 can of cola | about 40 mg | NHS |
| 1 can of energy drink, 250 ml | about 80 mg | NHS |
| Brewed coffee (355 ml) | 113–247 mg | FDA |
| Energy drink (355 ml) | 41–246 mg | FDA |

Vietnamese drip coffee (cà phê phin) and Vietnamese milk tea: **no official measurement verified**. A strong cup may contain more than instant coffee. [Inference]

> Hypothetical example: instant coffee in the morning (100 mg), a mug of tea at lunch (75 mg), an energy drink in the afternoon (80 mg). About 255 mg in total, below 400 mg. But the late-afternoon can may make it harder to sleep. [Hypothetical example]

## How to do it – the minimal version

**Step 1. Count one day.** Write down every caffeinated drink and add it up using the table above. Count a large cup as two. [Inference]

**Step 2. Have your last cup early.** If you sleep at 10 pm, have your last cup before about 1 pm (see [Minimal sleep, breathing and meditation](/en/methods/minimal-sleep-breathing-meditation/)). [Inference]

**Step 3. Don't use caffeine instead of sleep.** Tiredness from short sleep needs more sleep, not another cup. See [How much sleep](/en/sleep/how-much-sleep/). [Inference]

**Step 4. Watch the sugar that comes with it.** Milk tea, coffee with condensed milk and energy drinks are often high in sugar. See [Free sugars](/en/eating/free-sugars/). [Inference]

**Step 5. Cut back gradually.** For example, half a cup less each week, or swap one cup for water. [Inference]

## Common misunderstandings

- **"Energy drinks have less caffeine than coffee."** According to the FDA, a can of energy drink can contain about as much as a cup of brewed coffee. [Verified]
- **"I drink coffee in the afternoon and sleep fine, so it's no problem."** EFSA says 100 mg close to bedtime may shorten and disturb sleep in some adults. People differ in sensitivity. [Verified]
- **"Caffeine powder is just strong coffee."** The FDA estimates that about **1,200 mg taken quickly** (less than half a teaspoon of pure powder) can cause seizures. Australia and New Zealand (FSANZ) banned retail sale of pure and highly concentrated caffeine in December 2019. [Verified]

## Who should take care

- **Pregnant, trying to become pregnant, breastfeeding:** a lower limit applies (EFSA, NHS: at most 200 mg a day). The FDA advises talking with a health worker. See [Pregnancy and breastfeeding](/en/age/pregnancy-breastfeeding/). [Verified]
- **Under 18:** EFSA proposes 3 mg/kg body weight a day. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) advises against energy drinks for children and teens. This article does not apply to children. [Verified]
- **People prone to palpitations, anxiety, insomnia, high blood pressure, heart disease, or taking medicines:** the FDA notes that "too much" varies with medical conditions and medicines. Ask a doctor or pharmacist. [Verified]
- **People using gym pills or powders with caffeine:** add these to your coffee and tea for the day (FSA). [Verified]

## When to see a health worker

- You often get a fast heartbeat, palpitations, jitters, nausea, headache or poor sleep with caffeine (the signs of "too much" listed by the FDA). [Verified]
- You want to stop caffeine but find it very hard, or you take medicines and are unsure about interactions. [Expert judgement]
- Chest pain, seizures or fainting, especially after caffeine powder or high-dose pills: **call emergency services** (see [Find help](/en/find-help/)). [Expert judgement]

## Level of evidence

**Moderate.** The 400 mg reference comes from EFSA's risk assessment of pooled studies, mostly observational. It is a level that "raises no concern", not a target to drink up to. [Expert judgement]
