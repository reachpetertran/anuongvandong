Sitting too long and how to break it up

Checked: 2026-11-25Evidence: Moderate (mostly observational studies with consistent results)Ages: 18-39, 40-64Author: Peter Tran

Educational content, not yet reviewed by a health professional. It does not replace advice from a doctor who knows your health.

The site is written for Vietnamese readers; the Vietnamese version is the original. Quotes from Vietnamese documents are unofficial translations.

Key points

  • Sit less and replace sitting time with activity of any intensity, including light activity. Vietnam, WHO, the US, Australia and the UK all say this. Verified
  • WHO and the UK state that the evidence is not enough to set a sitting-time threshold. Vietnam advises people with heart disease or type 2 diabetes not to sit still for more than 60 minutes. Verified
  • About 60–75 minutes a day of moderate activity is associated with removing the extra risk of death linked to sitting a lot. Watching TV for 5 hours a day or more is still linked to higher risk. These are observational data. Verified

What the standards say

Standard Advice on sitting Any number?
Vietnam – Department of Disease Prevention (Decision 103/QĐ-PB, 13 Jul 2026) "Limit sedentary time and replace it with physical activity of any intensity" (section 2.3.2, p. 13). Workplaces: encourage 5–10 minutes of movement during working hours – stand up, rotate joints, stretch, practise breathing (section 4.3, p. 34) Healthy adults: no. People with heart disease or type 2 diabetes: do not sit still for more than 60 minutes (sections 2.6.2.2–2.6.2.3, pp. 20–21)
WHO (2020) Limit sedentary time; replace it with activity of any intensity. To reduce the harm of high sitting, aim for more than the recommended activity No – evidence was insufficient to quantify a threshold
US – PAG, 2nd edition The first key guideline for adults: move more and sit less No
Australia – 24-Hour Guidelines (2026) Limit sedentary time; break up long periods of sitting as often as possible; replace sitting (including recreational screen time) with activity No
UK – CMOs (updated 10 Jul 2026) Minimise sedentary time; break up long periods with at least light activity. The 2026 update cites one review: each extra hour of sitting is associated with about 5% higher risk No – evidence still does not support a specific limit

Sedentary behaviour means sitting, reclining or lying down while awake, using little energy (≤1.5 MET). Examples: desk work, driving, watching TV. Verified

What the evidence says

  • Ekelund 2016, The Lancet: a harmonised meta-analysis of data from more than 1 million people. In people doing about 60–75 minutes of moderate activity a day, high sitting time was not linked to higher risk of death. For TV time, this level of activity reduced but did not remove the extra risk. Compared with people who sat under 4 hours a day and were most active, the least active people who sat over 8 hours a day had 59% higher mortality. Verified
  • Ekelund 2020, BJSM: 9 cohort studies, 44,370 people wearing activity monitors. About 30–40 minutes a day of moderate-to-vigorous activity weakened the link between sitting time and risk of death. Verified
  • Both are observational: they show an association, not proof of cause. Verified

How to do it – the minimal version

Step 1. Set a reminder to stand up every 30–60 minutes. Stand, walk a few steps, get water, stretch for 1–3 minutes. The standards give no number of minutes; this one is just easy to remember. Inference

Step 2. Attach standing to things you already do. Stand or walk during phone calls. Stand in short meetings. Use a printer further away. Inference

Step 3. Use breaks for 5–10 minutes of movement. Stand, rotate your joints, stretch, practise breathing – exactly what the Vietnamese handbook suggests for workplaces. Verified

Step 4. Sit less in front of screens in the evening. Stand up between episodes. Do light housework while watching. Inference

Step 5. Keep your weekly activity goal. Breaking up sitting does not replace 150–300 minutes of moderate activity. See How many minutes of activity a week. Inference

Hypothetical example: an office worker on an 8-hour day sets a 45-minute timer and stands for 2 minutes each time, walks 15 minutes at lunch and walks briskly for 20 minutes in the evening. Hypothetical example

Common misunderstandings

  • "I exercised this morning, so sitting all day is fine." The UK states that reducing sitting brings extra benefit even for people who are otherwise active. In Ekelund 2016, about 60–75 minutes of moderate activity a day was needed to remove the risk linked to high sitting. Verified
  • "There is a safe number of sitting hours." WHO and the UK both say the evidence is not enough to set a threshold. Verified
  • "Standing all day instead of sitting is best." The aim is to change position and move. Standing still in one place for a long time is tiring too. Expert judgement

Who should take care

  • People with heart disease or type 2 diabetes: the Vietnamese handbook advises not sitting still for more than 60 minutes. Verified
  • People who cannot stand or who use a wheelchair: the sedentary definition still applies. Replace it with arm and upper-body movement as you are able; ask a health worker which exercises suit you. [Verified for the definition; the rest is Inference]
  • People who get dizzy when standing up: stand up slowly and hold on to a table or chair. Expert judgement
  • Pregnant women: separate recommendations apply; ask a health worker. Inference

When to see a health worker

  • Back or neck pain that lasts, or spreads into an arm or leg. Expert judgement
  • One calf suddenly swollen, painful and warm, especially after a long trip spent sitting. Expert judgement
  • Sudden shortness of breath or chest pain: call emergency services (see Find help).

Level of evidence

Moderate. The evidence comes mainly from large observational studies with consistent direction. A sitting-time threshold or the best frequency of breaks has not been established. Expert judgement
