Reading evidence: RCTs, meta-analyses, observational studies

Checked: 2026-11-25Evidence: Not applicable – a reading-method article; definitions from Cochrane, NHMRC, GRADEAges: 18-39, 40-64Author: Peter Tran

Educational content, not yet reviewed by a health professional. It does not replace advice from a doctor who knows your health.

The site is written for Vietnamese readers; the Vietnamese version is the original.

Key points

  • Observational studies only show that two things go together ("associated with"). A randomised controlled trial (RCT) can test whether one thing causes another. Verified
  • A systematic review gathers all studies on one question using a pre-set method; Cochrane calls it the most reliable way to find out whether an intervention works. Verified
  • Evidence is rated at 4 levels of certainty (the GRADE system): High, Moderate, Low, Very low. The site uses these levels in each article's "Level of evidence" section. Verified

Common study types

Type How it works What it tells you Example on this site
Observational study (cohort, cross-sectional) Follows people living normally and compares groups "Associated with". Groups may differ in many other ways (age, income, existing illness) Ding 2025: 7,000 steps a day is associated with 47% lower mortality than 2,000 steps. See 7,000 steps and VILPA
Randomised controlled trial (RCT) Assigns participants at random to an intervention group and a control group Whether the intervention causes a change Cochrane review 2019 (108 RCTs): exercise reduced the rate of falls in older people by 23%. See Strength and balance
Systematic review, meta-analysis Finds, appraises and pools all eligible studies using a pre-set method The overall picture; only as good as the studies it pools Most of the key figures on this site
Modelling Uses assumptions and data to estimate what could happen "If… then…" scenarios, not measured results EAT-Lancet 2025: "about 15 million deaths prevented a year". See Plant-based eating

Cochrane describes a systematic review as carefully examining all the research to answer a question, then using statistics to combine many studies, which makes it more reliable than single studies. Verified

Four levels of certainty (GRADE)

Level Meaning (paraphrased)
High Very confident the true effect is close to the estimate
Moderate Moderately confident; the true effect is probably close, but may be substantially different
Low Limited confidence; the true effect may be substantially different
Very low Very little confidence; the true effect is likely to be substantially different

According to the NHMRC (Australia), when judging the effect of an intervention, RCT evidence starts at High and observational evidence starts at Low; ratings can then move down or up. Cochrane rates down for risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. Verified

How to do it – 5 questions for health news

  1. What kind of study is it? Observational, RCT, review or model? Verified
  2. Who was studied? People, animals or cells? How many, and what ages? Inference
  3. Does the story say "causes" or "cuts risk by X%" from an observational study? If so, be careful. Verified
  4. What was measured? Real outcomes (heart attacks, deaths) or only an intermediate marker? Inference
  5. What do official guidelines say? WHO, the Ministry of Health, the NHS… weigh many studies together. The site always puts the "What the standards say" table first. Expert judgement

Common misconceptions

  • "If the news says it, the study says it." A Nutrition Society report (Proc Nutr Soc 2022) cites a study of 462 university press releases: 33% inferred causation beyond what the study showed and 40% contained exaggerated advice. Verified
  • "One big study is enough." A single study, however large, can be wrong; systematic reviews pool many studies and are more reliable. Verified
  • "Low-certainty evidence means it doesn't work." Low means not yet certain, not proven useless. Verified

Who should take care

  • People under treatment: don't change medicines or treatment because of one news story or one new study. Ask the doctor treating you. Expert judgement

When to see a health worker

  • When a health story makes you want to stop a medicine, make a big diet change or buy an expensive product: ask a health worker first. Expert judgement
  • When you have symptoms: get checked; don't let searching replace a consultation. Emergencies: see Find help.
