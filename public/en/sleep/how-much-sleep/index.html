How much sleep is enough

Checked: 2026-11-24Evidence: Moderate – High (expert consensus based on an evidence review)Ages: 18-39, 40-64Author: Peter Tran

Educational content, not yet reviewed by a health professional. It does not replace advice from a doctor who knows your health.

The site is written for Vietnamese readers; the Vietnamese version is the original.

Key points

  • Adults should sleep 7 hours or more a night, regularly. Verified
  • Australia recommends 7–9 hours of good-quality sleep with consistent bed and wake times. Verified
  • Regularly sleeping under 7 hours is associated with weight gain, diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease and depression. These are observational data. Verified

How to do it – the minimal version

Step 1. Work out your sleep window. Take the time you must get up and count back 7–9 hours. Add the time you usually take to fall asleep. Inference

Hypothetical example: you must be up at 5:30. Counting back 7.5 hours gives 22:00. Go to bed around 21:45. Hypothetical example

Step 2. Five habits that help sleep.

  1. Go to bed and get up at similar times every day, weekends included. Verified
  2. Avoid phones, tablets and computers for about an hour before bed. Verified
  3. Cut down on coffee, tea and caffeinated soft drinks in the afternoon and evening. Verified
  4. Avoid alcohol and nicotine for 1–2 hours before bed. Verified
  5. Be active earlier in the day if exercising close to bedtime keeps you awake. Verified

Step 3. Track two weeks. Note when you go to bed, when you get up and how you feel in the morning. Feeling alert during the day is a good sign. Inference

Common misunderstandings

  • "Catching up at the weekend is enough." Australia stresses regular bed and wake times, not just total hours. Verified
  • "The more sleep the better." AASM sets no upper limit, but notes that for healthy people outside the groups above it is uncertain whether more than 9 hours is linked to health risk. Verified
  • "A drink helps me sleep." The NHS notes alcohol can disrupt sleep. Verified

Who should take care

  • Night and shift workers: fixed times are hard. Ask the health worker at your workplace. Inference
  • Drivers and machine operators: daytime sleepiness causes accidents. AASM links sleeping under 7 hours with greater risk of accidents. Verified
  • People on medication or with a long-term condition: do not start sleeping pills or sleep aids on your own. Inference

When to see a health worker

  • You worry you sleep too little or too much: AASM advises talking to a health professional. Verified
  • Trouble sleeping lasts for weeks and affects your day. Inference
  • Loud snoring, someone sees you stop breathing in your sleep, or heavy daytime sleepiness. Inference

Level of evidence

Moderate – High. The 7-hour figure comes from an AASM and SRS expert panel consensus after a literature review. The links between short sleep and disease come mostly from observational studies. Expert judgement
