Myth: “Eating less salt just means less table salt”

Checked: 2026-11-25Evidence: Moderate – National Institute of Nutrition study (cited by WHO Viet Nam, 2014) and STEPS surveys 2015–2021 measuring urinary sodiumAges: 18-39, 40-64Author: Peter Tran

Educational content, not yet reviewed by a health professional. It does not replace advice from a doctor who knows your health.

The site is written for Vietnamese readers; the Vietnamese version is the original.

Key points

  • Myth: eating less salt just means using less table salt. [Verified as a common myth]
  • Evidence: according to a National Institute of Nutrition study (cited by WHO Viet Nam in 2014), 81% of sodium in the Vietnamese diet comes from condiments added during preparation, cooking and at the table; 11.6% from processed foods; 7.4% from natural foods. Seasoning powder and fish sauce are the main sources. Verified
  • Vietnamese adults aged 18–69 ate on average 8.07 g of salt a day (2021), above WHO's advice (under 5 g). Verified

What the standards say

Source Content
WHO (Sodium reduction fact sheet, 11 May 2026) Adults: under 2,000 mg sodium a day (under 5 g salt). Bread, processed meat and snacks can be high in sodium even if they don't taste salty.
WHO Viet Nam (27 Sep 2014) Advice for families: remove fish sauce, soy sauce and salt from the dining table; limit salty snacks.
Vietnam – Decision 3594/QĐ-BYT (2024), tip 7 Limit salty foods.
Lan et al. (2025), STEPS data Average salt intake fell from 9.42 g (2015) to 8.07 g (2021); still above recommendations. The study names salt, fish sauce, soy sauce and other sodium-based condiments as reasons Vietnamese diets are high in sodium.

For the US, Australian and UK standards, see Salt.

How to do it – minimal version

  1. Take the fish-sauce bowl and seasoning powder off the table, as WHO Viet Nam suggests. Verified
  2. Don't drink all the broth of phở, bún or instant noodles. Inference
  3. Read the sodium label on fish sauce and stock granules; multiply by 2.5 to get grams of salt. See the method in Salt. Verified
  4. Season a little less each week, and add flavour with lime, garlic, ginger, pepper and herbs. Inference

Common misconceptions

  • "Fish sauce and stock granules aren't salt." They contain sodium and, according to the National Institute of Nutrition, are the main sodium sources for Vietnamese people. Verified
  • "If it doesn't taste salty, it's low in sodium." WHO: bread, processed meat and snacks can be high in sodium without tasting salty. Verified
  • "Cutting salt out completely is best." The standards set an upper limit, not zero; WHO advises that the salt you do use should be iodised. Verified

Who should take care

  • People with kidney disease, or conditions that make it hard to clear potassium (for example some medicines): don't switch to potassium-based lower-sodium salt on your own; WHO excludes this group from its recommendation. See Salt. Verified
  • Heavy manual workers who sweat a lot, and people on a salt plan set by their doctor: follow your doctor's advice. Expert judgement

When to see a health worker

  • Repeated high blood pressure readings. See the blood pressure schedule on the Middle age 40–64 page. Verified
  • Severe headache, chest pain, difficulty breathing, weakness on one side: call emergency services (see Find help).

Level of evidence

  • Condiments are the main sodium source for Vietnamese people: Moderate. A National Institute of Nutrition dietary study (reported around 2014, needs updating); salt intake trends from STEPS urinary sodium surveys. Expert judgement
