Myth: “Juice detox”

Checked: 2026-11-25Evidence: Low – no RCTs have assessed commercial detox diets in humans (Klein & Kiat 2015 review); NCCIH (US) reaches the same conclusionAges: 18-39, 40-64Author: Peter Tran

Educational content, not yet reviewed by a health professional. It does not replace advice from a doctor who knows your health.

The site is written for Vietnamese readers; the Vietnamese version is the original.

Key points

  • Myth: a few days of juices, teas or "detox" pills will flush out toxins and help you lose weight. [Verified as a common myth]
  • Evidence: Klein and Kiat's review (2015) concluded there is no compelling evidence for detox diets for weight management or toxin elimination; no RCTs have assessed commercial detox diets in humans. Verified
  • The body already has its own waste-removal system: the liver, kidneys, gut, skin and lungs work continuously (British Dietetic Association – BDA). Verified

What the standards say

Source Conclusion
US – NCCIH (National Institutes of Health) Some detox studies had positive results but were low quality. A 2017 review: juicing and detox diets can cause initial weight loss through very low calorie intake, but weight tends to return on a normal diet. Colon cleansing has little evidence and can have serious side effects.
UK – British Dietetic Association (BDA) Unless you have a serious medical condition, the body removes waste itself. No pills, drinks, patches or lotions do it better.
Australia – Queensland Health Detox products don't detox or cleanse any part of the body; no credible research shows detoxes improve health conditions. Weakness and pain during a "detox" are likely dehydration, low electrolytes or too few calories – not "toxins leaving".
WHO, Vietnam MoH The site has not found specific recommendations on detox (not verified).

How to do it – minimal version

  1. Instead of a few days of "detox", change a few habits for good: less alcohol, fewer sugary drinks, fewer ultra-processed foods. See Alcohol, Ultra-processed foods. Inference
  2. Eat whole fruit rather than only drinking juice. WHO counts sugar in fruit juice as free sugar. See Free sugars. Verified
  3. Drink water as you need it. See Drinking water. Verified
  4. Don't buy "detox" pills or teas containing laxatives or diuretics. The 2015 review warns of the risk of overdosing on supplements, laxatives, diuretics and even water. Verified

Common misconceptions

  • "Losing weight after 3 days of juice means the detox worked." The quick loss is mostly from eating very little; NCCIH notes the weight tends to come back on a normal diet. Verified
  • "Tiredness and headaches are toxins leaving." Queensland Health: more likely dehydration, low electrolytes or too little energy. Verified
  • "'Natural' products are harmless." Some detox products contain laxatives; long-term use can cause harm. Verified

Who should take care

  • People with kidney or heart disease, diabetes, or on medicines: fasting, juice-only diets and diuretics can upset electrolytes and lower blood sugar. Ask a health worker first. Expert judgement
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding women, children, frail older adults: should not follow detox diets. Expert judgement
  • People with a history of eating disorders: detox diets are a form of extreme dieting. Expert judgement

When to see a health worker

  • Lasting diarrhoea, dizziness, a racing heart or cramps during a "detox". Expert judgement
  • Feeling you need to "cleanse" often because of worries about food. Expert judgement
  • Emergencies: see Find help.

Level of evidence

  • Commercial detox diets remove toxins or cause lasting weight loss: Very low. No human RCTs of commercial programmes; available studies are low quality. Verified
