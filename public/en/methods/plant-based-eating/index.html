Plant-based eating

Checked: 2026-11-25Evidence: Moderate–High for adults when well planned (AND 2025 position based on systematic reviews); Low for the '15 million deaths prevented' figure (EAT-Lancet modelling)Ages: 18-39, 40-64Author: Peter Tran

Educational content, not yet reviewed by a health professional. It does not replace advice from a doctor who knows your health.

The site is written for Vietnamese readers; the Vietnamese version is the original.

Key points

  • "Plant-based eating" means making vegetables, fruit, beans, nuts and whole grains the main part of meals. It does not have to mean giving up meat and fish altogether. Verified
  • Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics (AND, US, 2025): for adults, appropriately planned vegetarian and vegan diets can be nutritionally adequate and benefit cardiometabolic health. Verified
  • A vegan diet needs a reliable source of vitamin B12: B12-fortified foods or a supplement. Australia advises vegans to take a B12 supplement. Verified

What the standards say

Standard Main recommendations
WHO (Healthy diet fact sheet, 26 Jan 2026) Healthy diets come in many forms, built on four principles: adequacy, balance, moderation and diversity. Carbohydrates should come mainly from whole grains, vegetables, fruit and pulses. Everyone over 10 should aim for at least 25 g of naturally occurring fibre a day.
Vietnam – Decision 3594/QĐ-BYT (2024), tip 3 Eat fish, poultry and nuts daily; eat red meat in moderation. The Ministry has no separate vegetarian advice for adults (the site found none).
US – DGA 2025–2030 (p. 3); AND (2025) The DGA lists beans, lentils, nuts and soy as protein sources alongside animal foods. AND: well-planned vegetarian and vegan diets can be adequate for adults.
Australia – Healthdirect A balanced vegetarian or vegan diet can provide all the protein, minerals and most vitamins needed. Vegans should take a B12 supplement. See a dietitian before starting, especially for children or during pregnancy or breastfeeding.
UK – NHS Without planning, vegans can miss out on calcium, iron, vitamin B12, iodine and selenium. Use fortified foods or consider supplements for vitamin D, B12, iodine, selenium and calcium.

The standards agree: more plants is good, but the more animal food groups you drop, the more carefully you must plan. Verified

How to do it – minimal version

  1. Start from familiar meals. Add a bean dish (tofu, bean soup, peanuts, sesame) to the rice meal and cut back on the meat. Inference
  2. Vegetables at every meal. WHO advises at least 400 g of fruit and vegetables a day (not counting potatoes or cassava). See Salt to avoid salty pickles. Verified
  3. Choose whole grains where you can: brown rice, oats, maize. Verified
  4. If fully vegetarian: the NHS suggests iron from pulses, dark leafy greens, wholemeal bread, nuts and iron-fortified cereals; omega-3 from walnuts, flaxseed, chia seeds and rapeseed oil. Verified
  5. If vegan: have a reliable B12 source (fortified foods or a supplement). Ask a health worker about the right type and amount. The site does not give doses. [Verified for the advice; Expert judgement for not giving doses]
  6. Watch processed vegetarian foods (mock meats, vegetarian sausages, instant noodles): they can be high in salt and fat. Read the label. See Ultra-processed foods. Inference

Common misconceptions

  • "Vegetarians always lack nutrients." AND (2025) and Australia: well-planned vegetarian and vegan diets can be adequate for adults. The condition is "well planned". Verified
  • "Going vegetarian automatically makes you healthy." NHS: without planning, vegans can miss out on several micronutrients. WHO: the foundation of a healthy diet is minimally processed food low in unhealthy fats, free sugars and sodium – this applies to vegetarian food too. Verified
  • "Seaweed and mushrooms give enough B12." Healthdirect: some studies suggest nori contains active B12, but vegans are still advised to take a B12 supplement. Verified
  • "EAT-Lancet proved plant-based eating saves 15 million lives a year." That figure is a model estimate from the EAT-Lancet Commission (2025), not a trial result. Its proposed diet still includes moderate amounts of meat and dairy. Verified

Who should take care

  • Children, pregnant and breastfeeding women: Australia, the NHS and AND all call for special care. See a dietitian before a vegan diet for a child or during pregnancy. See also the By age pages. Verified
  • Older adults, people losing weight, with anaemia, kidney disease, or diabetes on medication: ask a health worker before a major diet change. Expert judgement
  • People with a history of eating disorders: cutting out several food groups may not suit you; have professional support. Expert judgement

When to see a health worker

  • Lasting tiredness, pale skin, breathlessness on exertion, numbness or tingling in hands and feet, especially after a long time vegan without B12. Expert judgement
  • Unintended weight loss after changing your diet. Expert judgement

Level of evidence

  • Vegetarian and vegan diets adequate and good for cardiometabolic health in adults: Moderate–High. AND based its position on systematic reviews; the condition is good planning. Verified
  • "About 15 million deaths prevented a year" (EAT-Lancet 2025): Low – a modelling result that depends on many assumptions. Expert judgement
