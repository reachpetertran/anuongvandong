7,000 steps and VILPA

Checked: 2026-11-24Evidence: Moderate (observational studies with a dose-response pattern)Ages: 18-39, 40-64Author: Peter Tran

Educational content, not yet reviewed by a health professional. It does not replace advice from a doctor who knows your health.

The site is written for Vietnamese readers; the Vietnamese version is the original. Quotes from Vietnamese documents are unofficial translations.

Key points

  • Compared with 2,000 steps a day, about 7,000 steps a day is associated with a 47% lower risk of death. Beyond 7,000, extra benefits are modest for most health outcomes. Verified
  • VILPA means short 1–2-minute bursts of vigorous effort within daily life: climbing stairs, walking very fast. About 3 bursts a day is associated with a 38–40% lower risk of death. Verified
  • Both come from observational studies: they show "associated with", not "causes". Verified

What the standards say

WHO (2020) No step target "Some activity is better than none"; all activity counts
US, UK The pages checked set no step target –
Australia – 24-Hour Guidelines, ages 18–64 and 65+ (2026) Companion statement (pp. 14 and 17): for those who are able and track daily steps, aim for 7,000 or more steps a day Gives stair climbing as an example that counts for both moderate and functional activity

Only Australia gives a step count, and it does so in a companion statement, not in the main recommendations. No standard mentions VILPA. Both are simple ways to reach the activity-minutes recommendation; see How many minutes of activity a week. Inference

What the evidence says

7,000 steps (Ding et al., Lancet Public Health 2025). - A systematic review of 57 studies (35 cohorts), 31 of them pooled in meta-analyses. Participants wore step-counting devices. Verified - Compared with 2,000 steps a day, 7,000 steps a day was associated with: 47% lower all-cause mortality, 38% lower dementia, 25% lower cardiovascular disease. Verified - Even 4,000 steps a day was associated with 36% lower mortality than 2,000. Verified

VILPA (Stamatakis et al., Nature Medicine 2022). - 25,241 non-exercisers in UK Biobank, mean age 61.8, wearing wrist devices for 7 days. Verified - At the median of 3 bursts a day (each standardised to 1–2 minutes): 38–40% lower all-cause and cancer mortality, 48–49% lower cardiovascular mortality. Verified - The authors state they cannot fully rule out reverse causation (people already ill may exert themselves less), and UK Biobank's response rate was very low (5.5%). Verified

How to do it – the minimal version

Step 1. Know where you are. Use your phone's step counter for a few days and take the average. Inference

Step 2. Build up. At 3,000, try 4,000, then 5,000. Hold each level for 1–2 weeks. In Ding 2025, each step up was associated with benefit. Inference

Step 3. Fit three 1-minute bursts into your day. Take the stairs instead of the lift. Walk very fast from the car park to work. Carry the shopping home faster than usual. Inference

Step 4. Keep strength work. Steps do not replace the 2 strength sessions a week the standards advise. Inference

Common misunderstandings

  • "Only 10,000 steps is useful." In Ding 2025 most of the benefit came by about 7,000; extra benefit beyond that was modest. Verified
  • "7,000 steps cuts my risk of death by 47%." That is an association from observational studies, not a promise for any one person. Expert judgement
  • "VILPA replaces exercise." The VILPA study looked at non-exercisers; it shows a way to start, not a replacement. Inference

Who should take care

  • People with heart disease, uncontrolled high blood pressure or lung disease: ask a doctor before sudden vigorous bursts (VILPA). Vietnam's handbook advises people with a condition to consider a check-up before starting. Verified
  • People with knee or hip pain, or prone to falls: fast stair climbing may not suit you. Inference
  • People who have been inactive for a long time: start at a level that matches your current fitness. Verified

When to see a health worker

  • Stop at once if you feel marked fatigue, dizziness, breathlessness or sharp pain, especially chest pain; seek care if it gets worse. Verified
  • Severe chest pain or fainting: call emergency services (see Find help).

Level of evidence

Moderate. Both are observational data with a consistent dose-response pattern. No randomised trial has directly tested the 7,000-step or 3-burst figures. Expert judgement
