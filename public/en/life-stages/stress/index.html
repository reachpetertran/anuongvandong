Stress

Checked: 2026-11-25Evidence: Moderate for self-help approaches (activity, mindfulness, CBT – per WHO, NHS, Australian Psychological Society); concepts and signs by guideline consensusAges: 18-39, 40-64Author: Peter Tran

Educational content, not yet reviewed by a health professional. It does not replace advice from a doctor who knows your health.

The site is written for Vietnamese readers; the Vietnamese version is the original.

Key points

  • WHO: stress is a state of worry or mental tension caused by a difficult situation. It is a natural response; everyone experiences it. Verified
  • A little stress helps us with daily activities. Too much stress can cause physical and mental health problems. Verified
  • How we respond to stress makes a big difference. Coping skills can be learned; WHO has a self-help guide that takes only a few minutes a day. Verified

What the standards say

Standard Main recommendations
WHO (Stress Q&A, 30 Mar 2026) Stress affects both mind and body. For many people it eases as the situation improves or as they learn to cope. The guide Doing What Matters in Times of Stress teaches practical skills, a few minutes a day.
Vietnam – Ministry of Health The site has not found official public guidance on managing stress (not verified).
US – MedlinePlus (NIH) Stress lasting weeks or months can harm health. Get help right away if stress leads to thoughts of suicide, feeling you can't cope, or using drugs or alcohol more than usual.
Australia – Healthdirect; Australian Psychological Society (APS) Stress becomes a problem when it overwhelms you or lasts a long time. APS: approaches shown to reduce stress include cognitive behavioural stress management, mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) and relaxation-based approaches.
UK – NHS Emotional signs: irritability, worry, trouble making decisions, racing thoughts. Physical signs: headaches, stomach problems, poor sleep. Long-term or severe stress can lead to exhaustion (burnout). Avoid using alcohol, cigarettes, gambling or drugs to relieve stress.

The standards agree: stress is normal, but when it lasts and overwhelms you, act – including getting professional help. Verified

Recognising the signs

  • Feelings, thoughts: irritable, tearful, worried, hopeless, hard to concentrate or decide. Verified
  • Body: headaches, muscle aches, stomach problems, poor sleep. NHS: stress sometimes causes high blood pressure and chest pain – if a symptom worries you, see a doctor. Verified
  • Behaviour: eating too much or too little, drinking or smoking more, avoiding people, dropping things you used to enjoy. Verified

How to do it – minimal version

  1. Name what is stressing you. Write it down: what can change, what can't. WHO suggests identifying the problem and its causes. Verified
  2. Plan for hard days. NHS: make a to-do list, plan your journey and what to bring. Verified
  3. Move. A 10–15 minute brisk walk when tense. See Active minutes. [Verified for activity; the minutes are Inference]
  4. Slow breathing or a few minutes of mindfulness. See Five minutes of slow breathing, Ten minutes of breath awareness. Verified
  5. Sleep enough, keep a fixed wake-up time. See Sleep habits. Verified
  6. Talk to someone you trust. Don't use alcohol or cigarettes to unwind. See Alcohol. Verified

Common misconceptions

  • "Stress is always bad." WHO, Healthdirect: a little stress helps us focus and meet challenges; the problem is too much for too long. Verified
  • "A drink takes the edge off." NHS advises against using alcohol, cigarettes, gambling or drugs to relieve stress; they can worsen mental health. Verified
  • "If I can cope on my own, I don't need help." NHS: if what you are trying isn't helping, see a health worker; talking therapies (such as CBT) can help. Verified

Who should take care

  • People with a diagnosed mental health condition (depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress): this article does not replace treatment; stay in touch with whoever is treating you. Expert judgement
  • People who have just been through a life-threatening event (accident, violence): severe acute stress can lead to post-traumatic stress disorder; get professional support. Verified
  • People with heart disease or high blood pressure: stress can worsen symptoms; don't assume chest pain is "just stress". Expert judgement

When to see a health worker

  • You feel you can't cope, or what you are trying isn't helping. Verified
  • Stress has lasted weeks and affects work, sleep or family. Verified
  • You are drinking or using drugs more to get through. Verified
  • Thoughts of harming yourself or suicide: get help now. Call emergency services or go to the nearest health facility; see Find help.

Level of evidence

  • Activity, mindfulness and cognitive behavioural stress management reduce stress: Moderate. The Australian Psychological Society lists these as proven approaches; certainty varies by method. See the evidence section in Ten minutes of breath awareness. Expert judgement
