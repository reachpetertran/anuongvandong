Drinking water

Checked: 2026-11-25Evidence: Low to moderate (recommended levels are based on observed intakes in healthy people, not on trials)Ages: 18-39, 40-64Author: Peter Tran

Educational content, not yet reviewed by a health professional. It does not replace advice from a doctor who knows your health.

The site is written for Vietnamese readers; the Vietnamese version is the original. Quotes from Vietnamese documents are unofficial translations.

Key points

  • Vietnam's Ministry of Health (Decision 3594/QĐ-BYT, 2024) advises "drink enough water every day", with no number of litres. Verified
  • The US and Australia give total water of about 2.7–3.7 litres a day, but this includes water in food. The UK advises 6–8 cups of drinks a day. Verified
  • Tea, coffee and lower-fat milk count too. A simple check: urine that is pale yellow and clear. Verified

What the standards say

Standard Adults Notes
Vietnam – Decision 3594/QĐ-BYT (29 Nov 2024), recommendation 4 "Drink enough water every day" No amount given
WHO No recommendation on how much water adults should drink found –
US – NASEM, Dietary Reference Intakes (2005) Total water: men 3.7 litres, women 2.7 litres a day An "adequate intake" (AI), not a minimum requirement. Includes water in food, beverages and drinking water
US – DGA 2025–2030 (p. 3) Choose water (still or sparkling) and unsweetened beverages No amount given
Australia – NHMRC, Nutrient Reference Values (2006) Total water: men 3.4 litres, women 2.8 litres; from drinks: men 2.6 litres, women 2.1 litres An "adequate intake" (AI)
UK – NHS, Eatwell Guide 6–8 cups of fluid a day Water, lower-fat milk and sugar-free drinks, including tea and coffee, all count

The numbers differ because some standards include water in food and others count drinks only. None is the single "right" figure. Inference

How to do it – the minimal version

Step 1. Drink regularly through the day. Keep a bottle on your desk or in the car. Drink when thirsty and with meals. Inference

Step 2. Check your urine colour. NHS: drink enough that your urine is pale yellow and clear. Dark urine means you should drink more. Verified

Step 3. Drink more in hot weather, heavy work and exercise. The NHS says you may need more fluid in a hot environment, and when pregnant or breastfeeding. Verified

Step 4. Choose unsweetened drinks. Water, unsweetened tea. Limit soft drinks; see Free sugars and sugary drinks. Verified

Step 5. Safe water. The Ministry of Health advises ensuring food safety when choosing, preparing and storing food (recommendation 8). Boil water if you are not sure of the source. [Verified for the recommendation; boiling is Inference]

Hypothetical example: an office day: 1 cup on waking, a 500 ml bottle at your desk in the morning and another in the afternoon, 1 cup with each meal. On a day inspecting a hot factory: drink more, and drink regularly. Hypothetical example

Common misunderstandings

  • "I must drink 2–3 litres of plain water." The US figures of 3.7 and 2.7 litres are total water, including water in food. Australia's drinks-only figures are 2.1–2.6 litres. Verified
  • "Tea and coffee don't count because they dehydrate you." The NHS counts tea and coffee as fluid; it only advises moderate caffeine. Verified
  • "The more water, the better." Drinking a very large amount in a short time can dilute blood sodium, which is dangerous. There is no need to force water when you are not thirsty and your urine is already pale. Expert judgement
  • "Drink lots of water to cleanse the body and lose weight." The standards checked make no such claim. Healthy kidneys adjust how much water you pass. Expert judgement

Who should take care

  • People with heart disease, kidney disease or cirrhosis, or anyone told to restrict fluids: follow your doctor, not this article. Expert judgement
  • Pregnant and breastfeeding women: fluid needs are higher. Verified
  • People working in heat and endurance athletes: drink regularly and replace salts; avoid drinking a very large amount at once. Expert judgement
  • Older adults: thirst may be weaker; drink on a schedule. Expert judgement

When to see a health worker

  • Strong thirst and passing a lot of urine for a long time without a clear reason. Expert judgement
  • Very dark urine, passing little urine, dizziness or exhaustion despite drinking. Expert judgement
  • Confusion, vomiting or severe headache after drinking a great deal of water or after working in heat; fainting: call emergency services (see Find help). Expert judgement

Level of evidence

Low to moderate. The US and Australia could only set an "adequate intake" (AI) – based on what healthy people drink – rather than a precise requirement. Real needs vary with climate, activity and body. [Verified for the AI definition; the general statement is Expert judgement]
