Free sugars and sugary drinks

Checked: 2026-11-24Evidence: Moderate (WHO recommendation; see the original for certainty grades)Ages: 18-39, 40-64Author: Peter Tran

Educational content, not yet reviewed by a health professional. It does not replace advice from a doctor who knows your health.

The site is written for Vietnamese readers; the Vietnamese version is the original. Quotes from Vietnamese documents are unofficial translations.

Key points

  • Free sugars are sugars added to food and drinks, plus the sugars in honey, syrups and fruit juice. Sugars naturally in whole fruit, vegetables and milk do not count. Verified
  • WHO: free sugars under 10% of total daily energy; cutting to 5% or less may bring extra benefit. Verified
  • Sugary drinks are the easiest place to cut: Vietnam's Ministry of Health and the US guidelines both name them. Verified

What the standards say

Standard Adults Notes
WHO (fact sheet 26 Jan 2026) Under 10% of energy; ideally 5% or less Expressed as % of energy, not grams.
Vietnam – Ministry of Health (Decision 3594/QĐ-BYT, 29 Nov 2024) Recommendation 7: limit foods high in sugar and sugary drinks The summary gives no gram figure for ages 18–64.
US – DGA 2025–2030 No amount of added sugars is recommended; no more than 10 g in one meal Avoid sugar-sweetened drinks such as sodas, fruit drinks and energy drinks.
Australia – Australian Dietary Guidelines (2013) Not yet verified (the source page could not be opened on the check date) NHMRC is reviewing the guidelines.
UK – NHS No more than 30 g free sugars a day (about 7 sugar cubes) Juice and smoothies: no more than 150 ml a day.

The US goes furthest ("no amount is recommended"); WHO and the UK set a ceiling. This is a difference in how the target is framed; the site does not pick a side. Inference

How to do it – the minimal version

Step 1. Change drinks first. Swap soft drinks, bubble tea and very sweet coffee for water or unsweetened tea. Inference

Step 2. Read the label. The "Sugars" (or "Total sugars") line includes the natural sugars of milk and fruit. Also check the ingredients: sugar, syrup, honey or malt near the top of the list signals a lot of added sugar. Inference

Step 3. Juice and smoothies count as free sugars. The NHS advises no more than 150 ml a day, drunk with a meal. Eat whole fruit instead of juice. Verified

Step 4. Lower the sweetness gradually. Use a little less sugar each week in drinks, sweet soups and braised dishes. Inference

Hypothetical example, only to show how the percentage works – not a target for you: someone eats about 2,000 kcal a day. 10% is 200 kcal. One gram of sugar gives about 4 kcal, so 200 kcal ≈ 50 g of free sugars; 5% ≈ 25 g. Hypothetical example

Common misunderstandings

  • "Pure fruit juice doesn't count." WHO and the NHS both count juice sugars as free sugars. Verified
  • "Honey or palm sugar is better than white sugar." The sugars in honey and syrups are still free sugars. Verified
  • "Just switch to sweeteners." The DGA 2025–2030 advises limiting non-nutritive sweeteners. Verified

Who should take care

  • People with diabetes: follow your doctor, especially if you take glucose-lowering medicine or insulin. Cutting sugar suddenly while on such medicine can cause low blood sugar. Inference
  • Children: have their own limits (e.g. NHS: ages 4–6 no more than 19 g a day). This article is written for adults. Verified
  • People on a prescribed diet: follow your health worker. The site gives no personal calorie or weight targets.

When to see a health worker

  • Strong thirst, passing a lot of urine or unexplained weight loss: get checked. Inference
  • You have diabetes and want to change how much sugar you eat.

Level of evidence

Moderate. WHO recommends under 10% and suggests under 5%. The certainty grade of each recommendation is in WHO's original 2015 guideline, which the site has not re-opened, so it is not stated here. Expert judgement
