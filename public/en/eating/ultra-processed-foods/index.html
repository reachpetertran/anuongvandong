Ultra-processed foods

Checked: 2026-11-25Evidence: Moderate (consistent observational data from millions of people; one small randomised trial)Ages: 18-39, 40-64Author: Peter Tran

Educational content, not yet reviewed by a health professional. It does not replace advice from a doctor who knows your health.

The site is written for Vietnamese readers; the Vietnamese version is the original. Quotes from Vietnamese documents are unofficial translations.

Key points

  • Ultra-processed foods are industrial products made from many ingredients and additives, with little of the original food left recognisable. There is no single global definition; research mostly uses the NOVA classification. Verified
  • The Lane 2024 review (BMJ, nearly 10 million people): eating more ultra-processed food is associated with higher risk across 32 health outcomes, especially cardiovascular, metabolic, mental health and mortality. These are observational data. Verified
  • The Hall 2019 trial: on an ultra-processed diet, participants ate about 500 kcal more per day and gained weight. The trial had only 20 people and lasted 4 weeks. Verified

What the standards say

Standard Content
Vietnam – Decision 3594/QĐ-BYT (2024) Recommendation 7: limit fried foods, fast foods high in fat, foods high in salt or sugar, sugary drinks and alcohol. Recommendation 2: read nutrition labels carefully. The term "ultra-processed" is not used. Verified
WHO Developing its first guideline on ultra-processed food consumption; published a provisional expert group list for comment (Nov 2025). No official recommendation yet. Verified
US – DGA 2025–2030 (p. 5) Avoid highly processed packaged, ready-to-eat foods that are salty or sweet, such as chips, cookies and candy; prioritise home-prepared meals. Limit artificial flavours, petroleum-based dyes, artificial preservatives and low-calorie sweeteners. Avoid sugary drinks. Verified
Australia – NHMRC 2013 guidelines: "discretionary" foods only occasionally and in small amounts. The revision includes ultra-processed foods in its evidence review. Verified
UK – SACN (2023, updated Apr 2025) The associations between higher ultra-processed food intake and poor health are concerning, but there are significant limitations in the evidence. UK government: more research is needed before concluding whether ultra-processed foods cause poor health. Verified

The standards point the same way: eat less packaged food high in salt, sugar and fat. They differ on whether to use the term "ultra-processed". Inference

Spotting them – the 4 NOVA groups

Group Vietnamese-style examples
1. Unprocessed or minimally processed Vegetables, fruit, rice, eggs, fresh fish and meat, beans, unsweetened fresh milk
2. Processed culinary ingredients Cooking oil, salt, sugar, fish sauce
3. Processed foods Tofu, canned fish, pickled vegetables, bread made from flour, yeast and salt
4. Ultra-processed Instant noodles, sausages, fizzy soft drinks, packaged snacks, industrial cakes and sweets

[Verified for the 4 NOVA groups; the Vietnamese examples are Inference]

Label tip: a long ingredient list with things you would not use when cooking at home (flavourings, colours, sweeteners, emulsifiers) usually means group 4. Inference

How to do it – the minimal version

Step 1. Change breakfast. Swap instant noodles or packaged cakes for sticky rice, bread with eggs, pho or home-made rice porridge – and go easy on salty broth. Inference

Step 2. Change drinks. Swap soft drinks and milk tea for water or unsweetened tea. Verified

Step 3. Change snacks. Swap chips and biscuits for fruit, roasted peanuts or unsweetened yoghurt. Inference

Step 4. Cook one more meal a week. Prioritising home-prepared meals is DGA advice. Start with one meal and build up. [Verified for the advice; how to start is Inference]

Step 5. Read the label before buying. Check salt (sodium), sugar, fat and the ingredient list. Verified

Common misunderstandings

  • "All processed food is bad." Cooking, baking, fermenting and canning fish are processing. NOVA keeps ultra-processed foods as a separate group; tofu and canned fish are in group 3. Verified
  • "It has been proven that ultra-processed food causes disease." Most of the evidence is observational. The UK concludes more research is needed before talking about cause. The Hall 2019 trial only measured food intake and weight over a short time. Verified
  • "Ultra-processed food is bad only because of salt, sugar and fat." In Hall 2019, the two diets were matched for energy, sugar, fat, sodium and fibre as served; participants still ate more on the ultra-processed diet. The mechanism is still being studied. Verified
  • "'High-protein' or 'low-sugar' products aren't ultra-processed." Marketing claims say nothing about the degree of processing; look at the ingredient list. Inference

Who should take care

  • People with a chronic condition (high blood pressure, diabetes, kidney disease) or on a therapeutic diet: ask a health worker before big changes. Inference
  • People with a history of eating disorders: sorting food into "good" and "bad" can make things worse; talk to a health worker. Expert judgement
  • Busy or low-income families: packaged food is sometimes the practical choice. Cutting back step by step still helps; there is no need for perfection. Expert judgement

When to see a health worker

  • You have a chronic condition and want to change your diet. Inference
  • You feel anxious or obsessed about "clean eating", skip meals or lose weight without meaning to. Expert judgement

Level of evidence

Moderate. Expert judgement

  • Lane 2024 (BMJ): umbrella review of 45 pooled analyses, nearly 9.9 million people. "Convincing" evidence for an association with cardiovascular death (RR 1.50); "highly suggestive" for all-cause death (RR 1.21), type 2 diabetes and depression. Many outcomes had low or very low GRADE certainty. Verified
  • Hall 2019 (Cell Metabolism): randomised crossover trial, 20 adult inpatients, 2 weeks on each diet. Ultra-processed diet: 508 kcal/day more eaten, 0.9 kg gained; unprocessed diet: 0.9 kg lost. Verified
