Primary school (6–11 years)

Checked: 2026-11-25Author: Peter Tran

Educational content, not yet reviewed by a health professional.

The site is written for Vietnamese readers; the Vietnamese version is the original.

Key points

  • Children aged 6–11 should do an average of at least 60 minutes a day of moderate to vigorous activity, with at least 3 days a week of vigorous and muscle- and bone-strengthening activity. Vietnam, WHO, the US and the UK agree. Verified
  • Sleep: AASM (US) advises 9–12 hours for ages 6–12; Australia advises 9–11 hours for ages 5–13. Verified
  • Australia advises no more than 2 hours a day of recreational screen time (schoolwork not included). WHO and Vietnam advise limiting it without a number of hours. Verified

What the standards say

Activity and screens Verified

Standard Activity Screens, sitting
Vietnam – Department of Disease Prevention (Decision 103/QĐ-PB, 13 Jul 2026), 5–17 An average of at least 60 minutes a day on all 7 days, moderate and vigorous; vigorous muscle-strengthening at least 3 times a week; bone-strengthening at least 3 times a week. Encourage walking or cycling to school Limit sedentary time, replace with activity of any intensity
WHO (2020), 5–17 An average of at least 60 minutes a day, moderate to vigorous, mostly aerobic, across the week; vigorous and muscle- and bone-strengthening at least 3 days a week Limit sedentary time, particularly recreational screen time
US – PAG/CDC, 6–17 60 minutes or more every day, moderate to vigorous; at least 3 days a week of vigorous, muscle- and bone-strengthening activity Not verified
Australia – 24-Hour Guidelines (2019), 5–17 At least 60 minutes moderate to vigorous every day Recreational screens no more than 2 hours a day (schoolwork excluded); break up long sitting
UK – CMOs (updated 2026), 5–18 An average of at least 60 minutes a day across the week; a variety of activities to develop movement skills, muscles and bones Reduce sitting and lying; break up with activity

Sleep Verified

Standard Recommendation
US – AASM (2016, endorsed by AAP) Ages 6–12: 9–12 hours per 24 hours
Australia – 24-Hour Guidelines Ages 5–13: 9–11 hours of uninterrupted sleep a night
WHO, Vietnam No sleep-hours recommendation verified for this age group

How to do it – minimal version

  1. Add up 60 minutes a day: walking or cycling to school if safe; PE lessons; outdoor play after school. [Verified for examples – Decision 103]
  2. 3 sessions a week with vigorous, muscle- and bone-strengthening activity: running, skipping, football, badminton, climbing. [Verified for examples – Decision 103, CDC]
  3. Set a limit on recreational screens (Australia: no more than 2 hours a day) and switch screens off 1 hour before bed. [Verified – Australia]
  4. Regular sleep times, 9–11 hours. Keep devices out of the bedroom. [Verified – Australia]
  5. Lunchboxes and snacks low in salt and sugar: water instead of sugary drinks, fruit instead of sweets. See Free sugars, Salt. Verified

Common misconceptions

  • "PE at school is enough." The standards advise an average of 60 minutes every day, all 7 days; PE is only part of it. Verified
  • "Computer schoolwork counts toward the 2-hour limit." Australia separates them: the 2-hour limit is for recreational screens, not schoolwork. Verified
  • "Tired schoolchildren can catch up on sleep at weekends." Australia advises consistent bed and wake-up times. Verified

Who should take care

  • Children with heart disease, asthma, epilepsy, a disability or under treatment: ask a doctor about suitable activity. The UK has separate guidance for disabled children. [Verified for UK guidance; Expert judgement for the rest]
  • Children who are overweight, underweight or have food allergies: need individual advice from a health worker. The site gives no weight-loss plans for children. Expert judgement
  • During activity, if a child is very tired, short of breath, dizzy or has chest pain: stop, rest and watch (Decision 103). Verified

When to see a health worker

  • Loud snoring, pauses in breathing during sleep, heavy daytime sleepiness, trouble concentrating at school. Expert judgement
  • Chest pain or fainting during activity. Expert judgement
  • Lasting sadness, withdrawal, bullying; abuse or violence against a child: call 111 (Vietnam's national child protection hotline). Emergencies: see Find help.
