Middle age (40–64)

Checked: 2026-11-25Author: Peter Tran

Educational content, not yet reviewed by a health professional.

The site is written for Vietnamese readers; the Vietnamese version is the original.

Key points

  • Most eating, movement and sleep advice for ages 40–64 is the same as for adults in general. Vietnam moves people aged 60 and over into the older-adult group for activity advice. Verified
  • What is new at this age is screening: many countries start offering heart checks and bowel and breast cancer screening somewhere between 40 and 50. The starting age differs by country. Verified
  • Muscle mass and bone mass decline gradually with age. Two strength sessions a week and balance training are the two habits most worth keeping at this age. Verified

Minimum daily dose

  1. Eating: use less fish sauce and seasoning powder, don't drink all the broth; swap sugary drinks for water or unsweetened tea. See Salt, Free sugars.
  2. Movement: about 30 minutes of brisk walking on most days. See Active minutes, 7,000 steps and VILPA.
  3. Keep muscle, keep balance: 2 strength sessions a week; a few minutes of standing on one leg and heel-to-toe walking 3 times a week. See Strength and balance.
  4. Sleep: 7 hours or more, with a fixed wake-up time. See How much sleep, Sleep habits.
  5. Know your screening schedule (section below) and have your blood pressure measured regularly.

What is specific to ages 40–64

Muscle mass declines gradually. According to Healthdirect (Australian Government), muscle mass starts to fall slowly from around age 30 and faster after 50. In women, muscle is lost faster around menopause. Strength training is the main recommendation, together with aerobic activity and enough protein. Verified See Strength and balance, Protein.

Bones start to thin. The NHS (UK) says bone is gradually lost from around age 35. After menopause, oestrogen falls and bone density can drop quickly. Early menopause (before 45) raises the risk further. Verified Weight-bearing exercise, strength exercise, not smoking and limiting alcohol are the NHS's osteoporosis prevention advice. Verified

Balance does not have to wait for old age. Australia (2026) advises balance and coordination activity at least 3 days a week for all adults 18–64. Vietnam and WHO advise this from 60 and 65. Verified Starting early is a simple way to prepare for older age. Inference

Menopause. It usually comes between 45 and 55. See Menopause: symptoms, what helps, and what the standards say about hormone therapy. Verified

Screening: what the standards say

Screening means testing people without symptoms. The tables only summarise each country's general programme; it is not a personal schedule. "Not verified" means the site has not found an official recommendation. Verified

Vietnam: a free yearly health check is now in effect. Under Directive 17/CT-TTg (6 May 2026), from 2026 everyone can have a free periodic check-up or screening at least once a year. The Ministry of Health issued guidance on its content on 12 May 2026. Adults get blood pressure, blood and urine tests and a chest X-ray; women get a gynaecology check that includes cervical and breast cancer screening. Ask your commune or ward health station for the schedule. Verified

Why countries differ: each programme weighs disease burden, resources and the balance of benefits and harms of the test. No single age is "right everywhere". Expert judgement

How to do it – minimal version

  1. Ask your commune health station or check-up provider what this year's check includes, and whether it measures blood pressure and blood sugar.
  2. Keep your blood pressure and blood sugar results in one fixed place (a notebook or your phone).
  3. If a parent or sibling had cancer or early heart disease, tell the doctor. Your screening schedule may differ. Expert judgement
  4. Keep up 2 strength sessions and a few minutes of balance training each week.

Common misconceptions

  • "I feel fine, so I don't need a check." High blood pressure often has no symptoms, which is why the Ministry of Health advises regular measurement. Verified
  • "If I have symptoms, I'll wait for my screening date." Screening is for people without symptoms. The Australian programme says people with symptoms or a family history of bowel cancer should see a doctor rather than use the screening kit. Verified
  • "After 40, weight training no longer works." Healthdirect names strength training as the main way to keep muscle mass. Verified

All articles for ages 40–64

Eating

Salt: the Vietnamese salty habit and how to cut back

Vietnamese adults eat about 8 g of salt a day, above the WHO limit. How to read a sodium label and 4 steps to cut salt in family meals.

Free sugars and sugary drinks

Free sugars are added sugars plus the sugars in juice and honey. WHO advises under 10% of energy, ideally under 5%. How to spot and cut them.

Ultra-processed foods

Eating a lot of ultra-processed food is associated with many chronic diseases, but the evidence is mostly observational and there is no agreed definition. What the standards say, and how to cut back in a Vietnamese-style diet.

Alcohol

WHO: no level of alcohol consumption is safe for health. Australia and the UK still set limits to reduce risk; the US dropped its numbers. Vietnam bans driving with any alcohol and bans drinking under 18.

Drinking water

Vietnam's Ministry of Health says to drink enough water every day but gives no number of litres. The US, Australia and the UK give different numbers because they count differently. How to check you are drinking enough.

Protein: how the standards differ

WHO, Australia and the UK advise about 0.75–0.83 g of protein per kg of body weight a day; the US 2025–2030 guidelines raised it to 1.2–1.6 g/kg, which is contested. Vietnam's Ministry of Health advises fish, poultry and nuts, and less red meat.

Caffeine: coffee, tea, energy drinks

Europe, the US and the UK use a reference of about 400 mg of caffeine a day for healthy adults. Australia sets no official safe level. No verified Vietnamese recommendation was found. How to count your intake and when to cut back.

Movement

How many minutes of activity a week

Vietnam's Ministry of Health, WHO, the US, Australia and the UK share one frame: 150–300 minutes of moderate activity a week plus 2 strength sessions. How to plan your week.

Strength and balance

Every standard says to do muscle-strengthening on at least 2 days a week. Australia also advises balance work on 3 days a week for adults aged 18–64. Home exercises with no equipment.

Sitting too long and how to break it up

Every standard says to sit less and break up sitting time. None sets a safe number of sitting hours for healthy adults. How to stand up and move during a working day.

Sleep and rest

How much sleep is enough

Adults should sleep 7 hours or more a night, with regular bed and wake times. How to work out your sleep window, plus 5 habits that help.

Habits that help sleep

A fixed wake-up time, stopping coffee early, avoiding alcohol and screens before bed, not lying awake too long. Good habits help, but they are not enough to treat chronic insomnia.

Breathing

Five minutes of slow breathing

Slow breathing at about 6 breaths a minute, 5 minutes a day. Evidence shows a small-to-moderate drop in stress and an immediate fall in blood pressure; long-term effects are unclear.

Mindfulness meditation

Ten minutes of breath awareness

A 10-minute secular mindfulness practice for healthy adults: notice the breath, notice when the mind wanders, gently come back. With 4 signs that you should stop.

Body and mind through life stages

Stress

Everyone feels stressed at times. A little stress helps us get things done; too much for too long harms health. The signs, what you can do yourself, and when to get help.

Menopause

Menopause usually comes between 45 and 55. Common symptoms, what helps, what the standards say about hormone therapy, and when to see a doctor.

Minimal methods

Plant-based eating

Every standard encourages plenty of vegetables, beans, nuts and whole grains. Vegetarian and vegan diets can be adequate if well planned; vegans need a reliable source of vitamin B12.

7,000 steps and VILPA

Two minimal ways to move: about 7,000 steps a day, and a few 1–2-minute bursts of effort in daily life. The evidence is observational.

Minimal sleep, breathing and meditation

Three small daily habits: a fixed wake-up time, 5 minutes of slow breathing, 10 minutes of mindfulness. Each has a different level of evidence, and some people should not use them.

Myths and facts

Myth: “Intermittent fasting beats ordinary dieting”

Two large reviews (BMJ 2025, Cochrane 2026) found intermittent fasting leads to similar weight loss as ordinary dieting – not more.

Myth: “Juice detox”

Your liver, kidneys, gut, lungs and skin remove waste every day. There is no convincing evidence that 'detox' diets clean the body or lead to lasting weight loss.

Myth: “Eating less salt just means less table salt”

In Vietnam, most sodium comes from seasonings added while cooking and at the table – fish sauce, seasoning powder, stock granules – not just from table salt.

Who should take care

  • People with heart disease, uncontrolled high blood pressure, diabetes, osteoporosis or joint pain: ask a health worker before increasing exercise intensity. Expert judgement
  • People with a long-term condition or taking medicines: the articles are written for healthy adults and may not apply to you. Expert judgement
  • People with a family history of cancer or early heart disease: the general screening schedule may not be enough. Expert judgement

When to see a health worker

  • Blood in your stool or a lasting change in bowel habits; a breast lump; unusual vaginal bleeding, especially after menopause. See a doctor now; don't wait for screening. Expert judgement
  • Weight loss without a clear reason, lasting tiredness, falls or worsening balance. Expert judgement
  • Chest pain, difficulty breathing, a drooping face, weakness on one side, fainting: call emergency services (see Find help).
